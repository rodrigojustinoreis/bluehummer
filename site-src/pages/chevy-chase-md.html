A century old, and worth the care.

Chevy Chase was one of the region’s first streetcar suburbs: deep front porches, real plaster, heart pine and oak floors, leaded glass and generous moldings. The kitchens were built for staff, not family; there is often a single original bath per floor; and basements have seven-foot ceilings. The work here is making the new rooms look as if they were always part of the house.

  • Chevy Chase Village and the Town of Chevy Chase. 1900s–1930s colonials and Tudors, many already extended once; projects often reopen a 1980s addition and reorganize kitchen, mudroom and family room as one plan.
  • Sections 3 and 5, Martin’s Additions, North Chevy Chase. 1920s–40s colonials and Capes on narrower lots where every inch of a galley kitchen or a five-by-eight bath has to work.
  • Rollingwood and Somerset. 1930s–50s brick colonials and Capes with slightly larger footprints and good bones for a primary-suite project.

What Chevy Chase homeowners ask us for.

New function inside an old house, without losing what makes it worth keeping.

  • A kitchen that belongs in the house

    Painted inset cabinetry, honed marble or soapstone, unlacquered brass, a bridge faucet under the original window, and the breakfast room or butler’s pantry folded in so the room finally has space to cook and gather.

  • Period baths that work today

    Small original baths rebuilt with hex and subway tile, a proper alcove tub or a glass shower, in-wall storage taken from a closet, and a second bath carved from a hall closet or a bedroom corner.

  • Headroom where there was none

    Lowering a basement floor or underpinning to gain ceiling height, then adding a family room, laundry, full bath and dry storage, with moisture managed before finishes go in.

Two permits are normal here, sometimes three.

County, municipality and, for some exterior changes, historic review.

  • Montgomery County DPS first. Structural and electrical work is permitted and inspected by the County’s Department of Permitting Services. Plumbing and gas permits come from WSSC Water.
  • Then the municipality. Chevy Chase Village, the Town of Chevy Chase, Section 3, Section 5 and Martin’s Additions each run their own building-permit or review process on top of the County’s, with rules on construction hours, parking, tree protection and dumpsters. We handle the paperwork and the neighbor notices.
  • Historic review where it applies. Parts of Chevy Chase sit in County historic districts, and exterior changes there need a Historic Area Work Permit. Interior remodels do not, but new windows, dormers or vents might.
  • What is behind the plaster. Knob-and-tube remnants, galvanized or cast-iron drains, balloon framing and undersized panels are common in 1920s homes. We open small inspection holes during design so the scope reflects reality.

Can you match the original trim, doors and hardware?

Yes. We profile existing casings and baseboards, source or mill matching trim, and specify period-appropriate hardware and lighting so new rooms read as part of the original house.

Do I need a permit from the Village or Town as well as the County?

For most projects, yes. Each Chevy Chase municipality has its own process, and some require neighbor notification. We prepare both applications and build the review time into the schedule.

Can you gain ceiling height in our basement?

Often. Lowering the slab or underpinning the foundation can turn a seven-foot basement into usable living space. It is structural work with a structural permit, and we assess feasibility and cost before design begins.

How far are you from Chevy Chase?

About 30 minutes from our base in Olney, down Connecticut Avenue or Georgia Avenue. We confirm the on-site schedule with you before construction starts.
